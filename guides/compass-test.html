The COMPASS test, explained by section

COMPASS (COMputerized Pilot Aptitude Screening System) is the aptitude battery used by flight academies and airlines across Europe, the Middle East and Asia to screen cadet pilot applicants. This guide covers what is actually on the screen, how the scoring works, and what preparation actually changes.

Who uses COMPASS

The test comes from EPST (European Pilot Selection & Training). You will meet it, or a close variant, at many European ATOs and cadet programmes, and at academies running selection for airlines in India, the Gulf and Southeast Asia. Some operators run the full battery, others pick sections and add their own interview or group exercises. If your invitation says COMPASS, expect most of the sections below.

The sections

1. Hand-eye coordination (tracking)

A joystick task. You keep a marker on a moving target, or fly a slalom through gates. The scoring cares about smoothness, not heroics: small constant corrections beat big late ones. Candidates who chase the target with aggressive inputs oscillate and bleed points.

2. Mental arithmetic under time

Speed, distance and time questions, ratios, conversions, percentages. The maths itself is secondary-school level. The difficulty is the clock: seconds per question, and the next one is already waiting. This is the section where most points are lost, because almost nobody has done timed arithmetic since school.

3. Short-term memory

Sequences of letters, digits or instrument readings shown briefly, recalled after a delay or a distraction. Capacity matters less than staying calm while items keep arriving.

4. Spatial orientation

You are given an instrument state (heading, attitude) and must work out where the aircraft is or which way it is turning. Trainable and very unfamiliar the first time.

5. Instrument monitoring

Gauges sit at the edge of the screen and drift. You must catch them going out of range while doing something else in the centre. Examiners call the underlying habit scan discipline: glancing at everything on a cycle instead of staring at one thing.

6. Multitasking

The section with no equivalent in everyday life: tracking with one hand while arithmetic and memory prompts fire, with monitoring on the side. Almost everyone collapses on their first attempt. This is normal and it is precisely what training changes fastest.

7. Personality and technical questionnaires

Depending on the operator: a personality inventory, sometimes basic physics or technical comprehension, sometimes an English test. There is no gaming the personality part; answer consistently.

How the scoring actually works

COMPASS results are normed. Your raw performance is compared to a reference population of applicants, and reported on a scale (typically 1–9, a stanine). A 5 does not mean 55%: it means you performed like the median applicant, and the median applicant is someone motivated who often prepared. Two consequences:

  • Untrained first attempts commonly land low. That says little about your ceiling.
  • Preparation moves you up the curve directly, because the curve is made of other candidates, most of whom prepare.

Why candidates fail

Rarely because the material is hard. The three killers are time pressure (easy arithmetic becomes brutal at seconds per question), divided attention (the multitask section punishes an untrained brain), and tilt: one miss snowballs into three because the candidate is still thinking about the last question. All three respond to training.

How to prepare

  1. Daily timed arithmetic. Ten minutes a day for two or three weeks beats a weekend cram. The goal is automaticity: 8×7 and 3:1 ratios with no thinking budget.
  2. Divided-attention practice. Any drill that makes you track and compute at the same time. The first sessions feel terrible; that is the adaptation you want.
  3. Fly the tracking calm. Small corrections, accept imperfection, never chase.
  4. Rehearse the format. Knowing what each section looks like removes the discovery penalty on test day.

FAQ

Is there a pass mark?

Operators set their own cut-offs and rarely publish them. Most look at the profile across sections rather than a single number, and weight the psychomotor and multitask parts heavily for cadets.

Can I retake COMPASS?

Policy varies by academy. Many impose a waiting period (often 6–12 months) before a retake, which is why arriving prepared the first time matters.

Is it an IQ test?

No. It measures speed, accuracy and workload management under time pressure, not general knowledge or verbal reasoning. High-IQ candidates regularly score low untrained, especially on multitasking.

How long does it take?

Plan for half a day. The cognitive battery itself is typically 2–3 hours; interviews or group exercises can extend it.
