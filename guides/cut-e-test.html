The cut-e pilot assessment, test by test

cut-e (now part of Aon) supplies the online "scales" tests used by many airlines to screen pilot applicants, including several major European carriers and cadet programmes. If your email says cut-e or Aon assessment, here is what is coming and how the scoring treats you.

How the cut-e format is different

Unlike a half-day COMPASS session at a test centre, cut-e usually starts online at home: a chain of short, sharply timed modules, each measuring one thing. Airlines pick their own combination, and top scorers are invited to a supervised retest, so inflating a home result is pointless. Each module is short — often 2 to 6 minutes — which means there is no time to warm up. You are either ready at question one or you donate points.

The modules you are most likely to meet

Memory (scales stm / mgt)

Sequences or grids of symbols shown briefly, recalled after interference. The interference is the point: something else happens between seeing and answering.

Monitoring and reaction (scales mt)

Watch several moving objects or gauges and react when a condition is met. Punishes staring at one spot; rewards a scan cycle.

Spatial orientation (scales nav, sr)

Headings, compass roses, instrument states: work out positions and turn directions quickly.

Applied numeracy (scales eql, tmt)

Short arithmetic and rule-of-three under a tight clock. Same story as every pilot battery: easy maths, brutal pacing.

Multitasking (scales mt variants)

Two or three of the above at once. The section candidates remember. Untrained scores here are uniformly poor, which is why it separates the field.

Complex control (ndb style)

Keyboard or joystick control of a cursor against drift, sometimes with inverted axes. Smoothness scores; frantic corrections do not.

Scoring: you are ranked against other applicants

cut-e reports normed scores: your result is placed against a reference group of applicants, not against an absolute standard. The consequence is the same as for every normed battery. The median is set by motivated people who mostly prepared, so an untrained attempt lands below where your ability says it should. Preparation is not cheating the test; the norm group already prepared, and the test assumes you did too.

Preparing in the weeks you have

  1. Automate the arithmetic. Daily timed drills until rule-of-three answers surface without effort.
  2. Train divided attention deliberately. It adapts fast, but only to combined practice, not to doing each skill separately.
  3. Practise instant starts. The modules are too short to warm up inside them. Start your practice sessions cold, first answer at full speed.
  4. Set up the real conditions. Quiet room, mouse if allowed, phone off. A single interruption in a 3-minute module costs a visible slice of the score.

FAQ

Do airlines see how many times I practised?

No. They see your scores on their modules. The supervised retest exists to confirm the home result, not to inspect your preparation.

Which airlines use cut-e?

The client list changes, but cut-e/Aon assessments are common across European flag carriers, low-cost carriers and cadet schemes. Your invitation email names the platform; that is the reliable signal.

Is cut-e harder than COMPASS?

Neither is harder; they stress the same abilities in different wrappers. cut-e's short modules punish slow starts more; COMPASS's longer session punishes fatigue more. Candidates who trained the shared mechanics report both feel familiar. One controller who sat both put it simply: the first was practice for the second.
