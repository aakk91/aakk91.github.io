The Aon (cut-e) assessment for job applications

If a bank, consultancy, airline ground role or graduate scheme has sent you an Aon or cut-e assessment link, you are about to meet the same "scales" test engine used to screen airline pilots — trimmed and remixed for corporate hiring. Here is what to expect and, honestly, which parts you can train and which you should prepare differently.

How it works

You get a link, a deadline, and a chain of short online modules, each 2 to 12 minutes, each measuring one thing. Employers pick their own mix. Strong performers are often re-tested under supervision later in the process, so there is no point gaming the home attempt. The defining feature is the format: modules are too short to warm up inside, and the clock per item is tight. You are either ready at question one or you donate points.

The modules you are most likely to meet

Numerical reasoning (scales numerical, eql)

Two very different things hide under this name. Data-interpretation tables and charts, where reading the question correctly is half the work, and fast applied arithmetic, percentages, ratios and rule of three under a short clock. The second kind is pure trainable speed.

Logical and deductive reasoning (scales cls, lst, ix)

Grids, sequences and rule-finding puzzles. Fluid reasoning under time: format familiarity helps, and pattern vocabulary builds with practice.

Memory and concentration (scales stm, e3+)

Short sequences recalled after interference, and long accuracy tasks where the challenge is staying precise at minute six. These come straight from the aviation side of the engine.

Monitoring and multitasking

For some roles (operations, trading floors, aviation-adjacent jobs): watch several moving things, react to conditions, sometimes while computing. Untrained candidates collapse here first.

Verbal reasoning and situational judgement

Passage-based true/false/cannot-say, and workplace scenario judgement. These are language and culture tests more than speed tests, and they need their own preparation: reading practice and understanding what the employer rewards.

Scoring: normed against other applicants

Like every product of this engine, your score is a rank against a reference group of candidates, not a percentage. The group is made of motivated applicants who mostly practised, so an untrained attempt undersells you. Preparation is not cheating; the norm assumes it.

What you can train, honestly

Straight answer, because prep sites tend to blur this: the speed mechanics are highly trainable — timed arithmetic, short-term memory, sustained concentration, monitoring and multitasking all respond to daily practice within two or three weeks. Data-interpretation, verbal reasoning and situational judgement respond to a different kind of preparation: practising the reading, not the reflexes. Most candidates over-prepare the reasoning and arrive with untrained speed, which is backwards given how the clock dominates these formats.

FAQ

Can the employer see my practice attempts?

No. They see the scores on their own modules. Supervised retests exist to confirm results, not to inspect preparation.

How long before the deadline should I start preparing?

Two to three weeks of short daily sessions beats everything else. The speed mechanics build like fitness; a single evening of cramming changes little.

Is this the same test pilots take?

Same engine, different mix. Corporate batteries lean harder on reasoning and judgement; pilot batteries lean on psychomotor and multitasking. The speed, memory and concentration cores are shared.
