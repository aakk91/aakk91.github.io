What a mechanical-comprehension question asks you to do

A technical diagram is a compact set of relationships. Before calculating, identify what is connected, what stays fixed and what the question actually asks. A gear-direction problem needs no speed calculation. A lever-distance problem needs moments, not a guessed proportional drawing. A pressure question needs the force and area in compatible units. This habit is useful in SPHAIR preparation and the DLR-related SWISS routine. The examples below are original practice, not official test questions.

Gears and belts: follow each connection

Two meshing gears reverse direction. An open belt keeps the direction; a crossed belt reverses it. Follow a chain one connection at a time. For speed, multiply the driving speed by driving teeth and divide by driven teeth. An intermediate idler does not change the ratio between the first and last gear’s tooth counts.

A 15-tooth gear turns at 120 rpm and drives a 45-tooth gear. What is the output speed?

120 × 15 ÷ 45 = 40 rpm. The larger driven gear turns more slowly. If the gears mesh directly, its direction is opposite. Direction and speed are separate questions.

Levers: force × distance from the pivot

For a balanced ideal lever, moments on the two sides match. Use the perpendicular distance to the force’s line of action. In the simple diagrams used here, the force acts downwards and the arm is horizontal, so the marked arm length is the relevant distance. Do not use the total beam length when the question gives distance from the pivot.

A 90 N load sits 2 m from a pivot. What effort balances it at 3 m?

90 × 2 = F × 3, so F = 60 N. A useful check is that the longer arm requires less force.

Pulleys: count supporting rope segments

In an ideal system with one continuous rope, negligible friction and negligible pulley weight, rope tension is equal along the rope. Count the segments supporting the moving load, not every vertical line in the diagram. More supporting segments reduce the effort, but you must pull more rope for the same lift.

Four rope segments support a 240 N load. What effort holds it, and how much rope lifts it 1 m?

The effort is 240 ÷ 4 = 60 N. Each supporting segment shortens by 1 m, so 4 m of rope must be pulled. These answers assume the ideal conditions stated.

Pressure and hydraulics: compare areas

Pressure equals force divided by area. If an ideal connected hydraulic system applies the same pressure to both pistons, the output force scales with piston area. Use area, not diameter: if a question gives diameters, area grows with the square of the diameter. Also check the units before substituting values.

A 50 N input force acts on a 2 cm² piston. The other piston has an area of 10 cm². What is its force?

The area ratio is 10 ÷ 2 = 5, so the output is 50 × 5 = 250 N. Both areas use cm², so their ratio is valid.

Springs, scales and how to review an error

Within the linear range, spring extension is proportional to force. Two identical springs in series each carry the load and their extensions add. In parallel, identical springs share the load. For scale drawings, a scale of 1:20 means each drawing length is multiplied by 20 to obtain the real length. Keep length, force and extension distinct in your working.
