The PILAPT test, explained

PILAPT (Pilot Aptitude Tests) is the screening battery you will meet at several UK and European flight schools and cadet schemes. It is shorter and more psychomotor-heavy than COMPASS or cut-e, which changes how you should prepare for it.

Where you will meet it

PILAPT comes from Symbiotics and is licensed to flight training organisations and airlines mostly in the UK, Ireland and Europe. It usually runs at a test centre or school open day, packed into a single sitting of roughly one to two hours, often alongside an interview or group exercise. Your invitation naming PILAPT is the reliable signal; the exact module list varies slightly by operator.

The subtests

Deviation indicator

The signature PILAPT task. A compensatory tracking exercise: a needle or marker drifts away from centre and you hold it back with a joystick. It measures smoothness and stability under drift, and it punishes overcorrection hard. Candidates describe it as deceptively simple for the first thirty seconds.

Hands and feet coordination

Two tracking tasks at once, one on the stick, one on rudder pedals or keys: your hands and feet follow different targets simultaneously. This is pure divided motor control, the thing flight instructors say drummers and gamers pick up fastest.

Number speed and accuracy

Short arithmetic comparisons and calculations against the clock. As in every pilot battery, the maths is easy and the pacing is the test.

Short-term memory

Sequences presented briefly and recalled under time pressure, sometimes while something else competes for attention.

Spatial awareness

Orientation problems from instrument or map-style cues, answered fast.

Capacity

The multitask module: tracking plus arithmetic plus monitoring in parallel. Like every combined test, it collapses untrained candidates and is the fastest section to improve with practice.

Scoring

PILAPT results are normed against an applicant reference group, and operators receive scaled scores per subtest plus an overall profile. Cut-offs belong to the operator, not the test. The practical consequence is familiar: the reference group prepared, so an untrained attempt undersells you, and format familiarity alone is worth real points in a battery this short.

Preparing for a short, psychomotor-heavy battery

  1. Prioritise tracking calm. With two joystick-based subtests plus capacity, half your result rides on smooth small corrections. Train yourself out of chasing the target.
  2. Drill timed arithmetic daily. Ten minutes a day, automatic answers.
  3. Train the combination, not the parts. Capacity only improves with combined practice: compute while you track.
  4. Arrive warmed up. A one-hour battery has no warm-up phase inside it. Do a short session the same morning.

FAQ

Is PILAPT easier than COMPASS?

It is shorter, not easier. The same abilities are tested with more weight on psychomotor control and less on breadth. Weak tracking hurts more on PILAPT than on any other battery.

Can I retake PILAPT?

Retake policy belongs to the operator. Many schools allow one retake after a waiting period; some count the first attempt only. Ask the organisation running your selection.

Do I need flight experience?

No. The battery assumes none and measures aptitude, not knowledge. Sim or gaming time helps mainly through the tracking and divided-attention habits it builds.
